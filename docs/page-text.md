## How Morse code works
Every character is a short pattern of two kinds of mark. The short mark is a dot. The long mark is a dash. In the recommendation, a dash is three times as long as a dot. The gap inside a character, between one mark and the next, is as long as one dot. The gap between two characters is as long as three dots. The gap between two words is as long as seven dots.
Those lengths are about timing, not about how many spaces you type. A page of text cannot hold a silent pause, so this translator writes the same structure with characters you can copy. One space means “the next pattern is a new letter.” The slash between spaces means “the next pattern starts a new word.” A line break in the text stays a line break in the Morse code, so a list or a short note keeps its shape.
Take the letter A. Its pattern is dot, then dash, written .-. The letter B is a dash followed by three dots, written -.... Put them in one word, AB, and the code is .- -.... The space in the middle is the letter gap. It is not part of either pattern. If you remove that space, the marks run together and become a different character, or no character at all.
Words work the same way, one step larger. SOS is three letters and no word gap, so it is written ... --- .... HELLO WORLD is two words. The code for HELLO, then / , then the code for WORLD. The slash is only a written stand-in for the longer gap. It is not a Morse character unless you typed a slash in the text. A typed slash is its own pattern, -..-., and it sits inside the word like any other mark.
Digits are five marks long (/morse-code-numbers/). Zero is five dashes. One is a dot followed by four dashes. Five is five dots. Six begins the return toward zero with a dash and then four dots. Because every digit has the same length, a number is easy to separate from a word of mixed letters once you know to count five marks.
Punctuation is less regular. A full stop is .-.-.-. A comma is --..--. A question mark is ..--... These patterns are longer than most letters, which is why a missing space changes the reading so quickly. The translator does not guess where a space should have been. It reads the groups you actually typed.
The same rule explains unknown groups. Eight dots in a row is the traditional sign that the sender made an error, but it is not one of the characters in the chart. This page shows it as a question mark and counts it, instead of inventing a letter. If you meant H and then E, type .... . with the space.
You can also type a few lookalike marks in the Morse panel. A middle dot or a bullet is read as a dot. An en dash, an em dash, or a minus sign is read as a dash. That only changes the Morse panel. In the Text panel, a dash character is the hyphen, whose own code is -....-.

## How to read dots and dashes
Read a pattern from left to right. A dot or a dash is one mark. Marks that belong to the same character are written with no space between them. When you write a word, a single space separates one character from the next. A slash, with a space on each side, separates words.
The letter A is a dot and then a dash, written .-. The letter B is a dash and then three dots, written -.... The word AB is .- -.... The space in the middle is the gap between letters. It is not part of A or B. Remove it and the marks run together into a different character, or into no character at all.
The translator follows that written form. One space in the Morse panel means the next group is a new letter. A slash, or a vertical bar, or three spaces in a row, starts a new word. Lowercase text is read as capitals. A line break in the text stays a line break in the Morse code.
## Timing in units
The chart shows shape, not time. Sound needs both. Timing is counted in units. A dot is one unit. A dash is three units. The gap between two marks inside one character is one unit. The gap between two characters is three units. The gap between two words is seven units.
Speed is words per minute. The standard word is PARIS, which is 50 units, including the word space after it. At 20 words per minute, one unit is 60 milliseconds. A dot then lasts 60 milliseconds and a dash lasts 180 milliseconds. The translator uses that scale when you press Play. Speed can be set from 5 to 40 words per minute.
Farnsworth spacing keeps the dots and dashes at the Speed you chose and lengthens only the gaps between letters and between words. Set Farnsworth below Speed to turn it on. When the two controls match, the Farnsworth readout says Off and the gaps stay the standard length.
## Learn the alphabet by sound
A chart is the right tool when you need to look a letter up. It is a weak way to learn to hear Morse. Counting dots on a page is slower than the sound, and the count falls apart once the speed rises. Learn the rhythm of each character instead of a picture of marks.
Keep the character speed steady and let the gaps stay longer while a letter is new. That is what Farnsworth spacing is for. Choose a Speed you want the marks to have, then move Farnsworth below it. The dots and dashes stay quick. The silence between letters grows, so you can name the character before the next one starts. Bring Farnsworth back up only after the rhythm is familiar.
Practice a few letters at a time. Many sound-based plans start with two characters and add another only after those two are easy to recognize by ear. Play them in the translator, look away from this chart, and write down what you hear. A letter you can recite from the table but cannot pick out of a short run is not learned yet.

## The five-symbol pattern
Count the marks in any row. There are always five. One is a dot and four dashes. Two is two dots and three dashes. The dots stay in front and the dashes fill whatever is left, until five, which is five dots and no dashes.
Six starts the return trip. It is one dash and four dots. Seven is two dashes and three dots. The dashes stay in front this time, and the dots fill the rest. Nine is four dashes and one dot. Zero is five dashes. Five and zero are the two easy ends: all dots, then all dashes.
Take the pattern for 1 to 5 and swap every dot for a dash and every dash for a dot, and you get 6 to 0: 1 .---- becomes -.... (6), 2 becomes 7, 3 becomes 8, 4 becomes 9, and 5 (all dots) becomes 0 (all dashes). So if you know 1 to 5 you already know 6 to 0.
Timing does not change for digits. A dot is one unit, a dash is three, and the gap inside the digit is one unit. Because a digit is five marks, it takes longer to send than a short letter such as E or T. The gap after the digit is still the normal gap between characters, three units, unless the digit ends a word.
## Numbers inside a message
Treat each digit as a character. In the written Morse this site uses, characters in one word are separated by a single space. The number 10 is the digit 1, a space, then the digit 0: .---- -----. There is no slash inside the number. A slash would start a new word.
A longer number works the same way. 2026 is four digits in one word, with a space between each pattern. If a sentence has a number and then a word, the slash goes between the number and the word, just as it goes between any two words. HELLO 73 is the letters of HELLO, then / , then the two digits.
The same five-mark rule helps when you are reading code you did not write. A group of five marks is a digit. A shorter group is a letter or a punctuation mark. You still need the spaces. Without them, five dots could be the digit 5 or a run of E letters, and the translator will not guess which one you meant.

# SOS in Morse Code
SOS is the distress signal in Morse code. The pattern is three dots, three dashes, and three dots: ... --- .... It is sent as one signal, in a single run. It is not three words, and it is not three separate transmissions.
## The sequence
Write the groups from left to right. Three dots, then three dashes, then three dots again. On this site the translator encodes the text SOS as one word, so the written Morse has a space between the groups and no slash. A slash would mean a new word. SOS is not S, then a word gap, then O, then a word gap, then S.
The translator plays SOS with the normal gaps between letters so the three groups are easy to read and hear. A sent distress call runs the three groups together without those letter gaps.
The distress signal itself is a prosign. A prosign is sent as one unbroken run, not as three separate letters with a stop between them. You do not finish S, wait as though the message had ended, and then start O. The three groups follow one another as a single signal. The spaces in the written form ... --- ... let a reader see those groups. They are not three messages.
A dot is one unit long and a dash is three units. Inside a group, the gap between marks is one unit. People learn the rhythm as three short, three long, three short, rather than as a sentence to spell. If you only remember one pattern from the alphabet, this is the one used to call for help.
## What the signal means
SOS is not an abbreviation. “Save Our Souls” and “Save Our Ship” are popular backronyms. They were fitted to the letters later. They are not the origin of the signal. The letters were chosen because the pattern is unmistakable: three short marks, three long marks, three short marks, with no extra word break in the middle.
The distress signal was adopted by the 1906 International Radiotelegraph Convention in Berlin and took effect in 1908. That is the decision that made this pattern the international call for help in radiotelegraphy.
Hearing or reading the pattern means the sender is in distress and needs help. It is not a greeting, a test word, or a signature. Practice it so you can recognize it. Do not send it as a joke or as a way to try the translator’s sound on the air.
## How people signal it
Any way of making a short mark and a long mark can carry the pattern. A short sound is a dot and a long sound, about three times as long, is a dash. A whistle, a horn, or a tapped coin on a pipe can do that. The same idea works with light: a short flash, then a long flash. It also works by knocking, with a short knock and a longer knock. The listener needs the rhythm of three short, three long, and three short, sent as one run.
Repeat the run if you can, with a pause between repeats so a listener can tell where one signal ends. Stay with the same short and long lengths. Mixing in other letters makes the call harder to recognize.
In a real emergency, contact your local emergency services. Use the phone, radio, or other help that is actually available where you are. This page explains the signal for learning. It does not send a rescue call, and playing it in the translator does not alert anyone.
